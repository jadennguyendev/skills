# History documentary

Use when: the video tells real past events, a real person's life, a real place's past, or how something came to be, from the record. Combines with: explainer (a mechanism taught through a historical case: explainer structure, this file's sourcing and no-invention rules), true-crime (a historical crime: true-crime's victim and sensitivity rules, this file's archive rules), horror (a documented disaster or atrocity told with dread: the dread comes from the record, never from invented detail), comedy (light history: comedy sets the voice, but no joke puts invented words in a real person's mouth). Fiction does not combine: a dramatised story with invented scenes uses the fiction file and says so on screen.

The viewer should feel they stood inside a moment whose outcome was still open, and leave trusting every sentence. The core craft file builds clarity and curiosity; this genre adds a contract with the record: the narration may arrange, select and juxtapose, but it may not supply a fact, a quote, a thought or even the weather that the sources do not.

## Principles

- **Turn every page, then find the gap** - Robert Caro. His editor Alan Hathway told him "Turn every page. Never assume anything." Caro's angle on Robert Moses came from the gap between the textbook (power comes from the ballot box) and what the record showed.
  - In a narration script: research before the X/Y line. Y is often the place where the familiar version and the documents disagree: "Robert Moses was never elected to anything. For forty-four years he had more power than any mayor of New York."
  - Sources: [Caro, "The Secrets of Lyndon Johnson's Archives", The New Yorker (2019), part I](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2019/01/28/the-secrets-of-lyndon-johnsons-archives); [Caro, The Art of Biography No. 5, Paris Review 216 (2016)](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/6442/the-art-of-biography-no-5-robert-caro)
- **Distill: discard the irrelevant** - Barbara Tuchman. The historian's first duty to the reader is to distill: select the essential, "above all, discard the irrelevant", and build "a developing dramatic narrative". The test of the artist is to resist the fascinating byways.
  - In a narration script: write each chapter's single thread in brief.md and park every good byway in a "cut" list. If an omission could mislead, say it once and move on: "The war in the east is its own story. This one stays on the road to Paris."
  - Source: [Tuchman, "In Search of History" (Radcliffe, 1963), in Practicing History (1981)](https://archive.org/details/practicinghistortuc00tuch) ([publisher excerpt](https://www.abebooks.com/9780345303639/Practicing-History-Selected-Essays-Tuchman-0345303636/plp))
- **Arrange to reveal, not just describe** - John Grierson. "Lecture films" describe and even expose, but "only rarely reveal"; documentary passes from description to "arrangements, rearrangements, and creative shapings". He faults the city film where "nothing having happened and nothing positively said about anything", the day ends.
  - In a narration script: a chapter of atmosphere (dawn, markets, carts, church bells) is the trap. Each chapter places two documented details side by side so they mean something together, and states the turn they produce.
  - Source: [Grierson, "First Principles of Documentary" (1932), in Grierson on Documentary, pp. 146-150](https://torch.ox.ac.uk/sitefiles/grierson-first-principles-of-documentary.pdf)
- **No one lived in the past** - David McCullough. People then "lived in the present", caught up in it "with no more certainty of how things would turn out than we have." History teaches that "nothing ever had to happen as it did." His questions: "Of what were they afraid? What didn't they know?"
  - In a narration script: narrate each decision from the hour it was made, and say aloud what the person could not yet know: "On the morning of the vote, neither side had counted enough votes to be sure." Hindsight belongs in the brief, not in the scene.
  - Source: [McCullough, "The Course of Human Events", Jefferson Lecture, NEH (2003)](https://www.neh.gov/about/awards/jefferson-lecture/david-mccullough-biography)
- **Suspense when the ending is known** - Erik Larson. For Dead Wake he set out to bring "real-life suspense": he tracked the submarine's war log and the liner's course side by side. However well readers know the ending, told richly enough "people suspend that knowledge."
  - In a narration script: cross-cut the two lines that will meet, each with its own clock, and let the viewer hope against what they know. "The Lusitania left New York on the first of May. The submarine that sank her had left its German base the day before."
  - Source: [Larson, interview, Asheville Citizen-Times (2016)](https://www.citizen-times.com/story/life/books/2016/03/19/asheville-lands-dead-wake-author-erik-larson/81875472/)
- **Make them see the scene, through the person who saw it** - Robert Caro: "Are you making the reader see the scene?" The facts alone are not enough. To show how hard Lyndon Johnson worked, he went back again and again to Johnson's driver, a source important "because of what they saw", not because of status.
  - In a narration script: for every scene, brief.md names the witness and the document. The narration takes that vantage: "Only his driver saw what he did between towns. In the back seat, Johnson went over every stop he thought he had lost."
  - Source: [Caro, Paris Review (2016)](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/6442/the-art-of-biography-no-5-robert-caro)
- **Rhythm, mood and place carry meaning** - Robert Caro: "Rhythm matters. Mood matters. Sense of place matters." He ordered Moses's expressways so the rhythm builds, then broke it in the last sentence, and kept a card on his lamp: "Is there desperation on this page?"
  - In a narration script: give each chapter one mood word in brief.md and read the chapter aloud against it. A roll of documented names can build over a few sentences, then a short plain sentence breaks the pattern and lands the point.
  - Source: [Caro, Paris Review (2016)](https://www.theparisreview.org/interviews/6442/the-art-of-biography-no-5-robert-caro)
- **Let the record speak, in its own words** - Ken Burns asks why use only a "voice-of-God narrator" when first-person voices can read newspaper accounts, letters or diaries. For ordinary people, he says, sometimes only a gravestone, an enlistment roll or a letter proves they lived.
  - In a narration script: one TTS voice reads the quotes, so name the writer and the document first and let a narrator sentence after it make the quote's end audible: "In his letters, John Adams addressed his wife as Dearest Friend. She called him the tenderest of husbands." Verbatim only, short, at most two per chapter.
  - Sources: [Burns, The Interviews, Television Academy Foundation (2007)](https://interviews.televisionacademy.com/interviews/ken-burns); [Burns, Throughline, NPR (2026)](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5677336)
- **Keep the viewer inside the era** - Erik Larson: anything that pulls the reader out of "the nonfiction dream" is bad. In Dead Wake he never mentions World War II, since none had happened yet, and avoids modern terms such as computer talk.
  - In a narration script: inside a scene, no later events and no present-day idiom ("rebooted his strategy", "went viral", "the first world war" before there was a second). Present-day framing goes only at a chapter's edge, marked as now.
  - Source: [Larson, interview, Asheville Citizen-Times (2016)](https://www.citizen-times.com/story/life/books/2016/03/19/asheville-lands-dead-wake-author-erik-larson/81875472/)
- **Invent nothing, not even the weather** - Barbara Tuchman: "I do not invent anything, even the weather." We were not there, so "the least we can do is to stay within the evidence." The summer thunder in The Guns of August was in the record. Erik Larson: imagining conversations is where you "cross the line into fiction".
  - In a narration script: every quote, thought, gesture and sky comes from a source in brief.md. Where the record is silent, say so or infer in the open: "His letters from that week are short. He does not mention the vote."
  - Sources: [Tuchman, "In Search of History", Practicing History](https://archive.org/details/practicinghistortuc00tuch); [Larson, interview with Joy Horowitz for Goodreads (2015)](http://joyhorowitz.com/Essays-and-Articles/interview-with-erik-larson.html)
- **Label the reconstruction** - BBC Editorial Guidelines 3.4.25-3.4.32: reconstructions rest on "a substantial and verifiable body of evidence", and it must be obvious where one begins and ends. Synthetic content must not distort the meaning of events, archive must not mislead, and commentary must never give a materially misleading impression.
  - In a narration script: every Opuscar picture is a reconstruction. The narration never calls it a photograph, film or document ("In this photo, taken that morning"), and never leans on an image to carry a claim the sources do not make.
  - Source: [BBC Editorial Guidelines, Section 3: Accuracy, 3.4.25-3.4.32](https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines)
- **Let it rhyme; do not point** - Ken Burns: events never repeat but they rhyme, and the filmmaker's discipline "is not to point signs" at the present; doing so dates the film and drops the complexity.
  - In a narration script: tell the past fully enough that the viewer hears the echo alone. No closing line that names today's parallel; the last chapter ends on the past's own objects and people.
  - Source: [Burns, Throughline, NPR (2026)](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5677336)

## Overrides to craft_principles.md

- Lead with the fact that sounds untrue: keep, without Herzog's licence. His "ecstatic truth" through fabrication never applies here; the strange fact must be in the record.
- Suspense over surprise: change. The viewer often knows the outcome already, so the bomb is already on the table; suspense comes from what the participants did not know and from a stated clock (McCullough, Larson).
- Because, not after: change. Chronology is often the right spine, because people lived events in order. Dates may anchor chapters, but every hand-off still joins with "therefore" or "but".
- Coincidence may cause trouble, never solve it: does not apply as a writing rule. Real history has lucky escapes; report them as luck, with the source, and do not dress them up as cause.
- A stand-in who knows as little as the viewer: change. The stand-in is a real, documented participant who was new to the situation (a recruit, a reporter, a visitor), never an invented villager.
- Make me care, through people: tighten. The person is named in the sources; a representative figure ("a private in the Fifth Battalion") is allowed only when brief.md names who it is.
- Leave room for two truths: keep, and extend to historians. Where they disagree, name the camps and what each rests on; never settle it with "historians agree".
- Begin with the wrong idea: keep. Popular history is full of myths; state the familiar version fairly, then the documents.
- Simplify on purpose, and say so once: tighten. Log omitted fronts, people and years in brief.md, and name the largest omission once in the narration (see Distill).
- Add to the picture: tighten. Opuscar invents its shots, so the narration carries every claim and never refers to an image as a record.
- Pay off the hook in its own terms, then stop: keep, with one addition: a single aftermath sentence (what became of the people) may follow the payoff, then the CTA.

## Brief additions

- Sources: per chapter, every claim mapped to its source (author, title, archive or collection, date). Mark each source primary or secondary.
- Quote ledger: every spoken quote verbatim, with speaker, document, date and source. A quote not in the ledger does not go in the script.
- Witness per scene: who saw it, and the document that records what they saw.
- Reconstruction note: which visuals are reconstruction (all Opuscar output) and which, if any, are real archive. No generated image may pass as a photograph, painting or document of the period; real people are not rendered so lifelike the picture could be mistaken for a record.
- Period guard: words, comparisons and later events the narration must not use inside scenes.
- Contested points: where historians disagree, who holds which view, and how the script frames it.
- Omissions and simplifications: what was left out and why.
- Mood word per chapter (Caro's card).
- Sensitivity: deaths, atrocity, people with living relatives, images of the dead, recent events. Add a content warning in the video description when the subject needs one.

## QA overrides

| Rule (narration-profile.md) | Change for this genre | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Invented specifics (P1) | Tighten: an invented quote, dialogue line, inner thought or weather detail is P0; other specifics missing from brief.md stay P1 | Truth is the genre's contract (Tuchman, Larson) |
| Vague attribution | Tighten: "historians say", "many believe", "legend has it" without a named source is P1 | The viewer must be able to judge the source (BBC 3.4.20) |
| Same-opener runs (P2) | Relax: one deliberate enumeration per video (up to four sentences of documented names or acts, broken by a plain sentence) is allowed | Caro's built list carries scale the ear can feel |
| Speculative openers | Tighten: second-person time travel ("You are a soldier in nineteen sixteen") counts as a speculative opener (P1) | It supplies sensations no source records |
| Rhetorical questions | Keep relaxed, but a question about a person's inner state must be answered from the record in the same chapter, or it is P1 | An unanswered "What was he thinking?" is speculation |
| Hindsight phrasing | Add: "little did he know" and similar irony (P1) | Breaks McCullough's present tense of the past |
| Present-day parallel ending | Add: "Sound familiar?" or a closing line naming today's parallel (P1) | Burns: do not point signs |
| Image claims | Add: narration calling a reconstruction a photo, film or document (P0) | Materially misleads (BBC 3.4.26, 3.4.32) |
| Spoken mechanics: dates | Dates written as read ("nineteen fifteen"); a full day-month-year at most once per chapter (P2) | The ear loses stacked dates |
| CTA | Keep in the last chapter; after deaths or atrocity, plain and short, never cheerful | Tone after loss |

Genre clichés to flag as P1: "little did they know", "history repeats itself", "sound familiar?", "the rest is history", "changed the course of history forever", "a turning point in history", "a watershed moment", "lost to history", "the world would never be the same", "a dark chapter in history", "the untold story", "shrouded in mystery", "against all odds", "echoes to this day", "a fateful day"
