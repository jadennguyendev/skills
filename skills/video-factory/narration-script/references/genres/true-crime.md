# True crime

Use when: the video narrates a real crime, investigation, trial, wrongful conviction, unsolved case, disappearance, scam, fraud or heist. Combines with: explainer (how a forensic method, a court rule or a scam's psychology works, never the step-by-step method), history-documentary (cases old enough that archives replace interviews; the naming and harm rules still apply to descendants and renamed people), horror (mood for an unsolved case only, never horror's invented scenes or a killer's point of view), comedy (only for capers where no one was hurt, aimed at the scheme or the institution, never at the victim or the mark). Not fiction: a story "inspired by" a case is written as fiction with every name and identifying detail changed.

The viewer should leave knowing what happened, how people came to believe what they believed, and what is still unknown, and should have cared about the people it happened to. Unlike the core explainer craft, every sentence is a claim about real, often living people: accuracy, attribution and harm limit what the story may do, and the ending goes where the evidence goes.

## Principles
- **Start from a question you cannot answer yet** - Sarah Koenig (Serial): "Don't make a story you don't actually have any questions about." Errol Morris put one question at the center of The Thin Blue Line: how did it happen that the man who did it walked free and the man who didn't was sentenced to death?
  - In a narration script: the X/Y line names a question about how or why, not a monster. "A video about a Dallas police shooting in 1976, and what's interesting is how a town convicted the hitchhiker instead of the driver." "...and what's interesting is how evil he was" fails.
  - Sources: [Koenig, DW Akademie interview (2025)](https://akademie.dw.com/en/serials-sarah-koenig-on-the-essentials-of-great-audio-storytelling/a-73777209); [Morris, Transom interview (2003), "Stories About Belief"](https://transom.org/2003/errol-morris-conversation/)
- **Know whose story you are telling** - Janet Malcolm: the subject who confides in a writer learns too late that the writer "always intended to write a story of his own."
  - In a narration script: decide before the outline whose account carries each chapter (the victim's family, the investigator, the accused, the record) and say once where it comes from: "Everything he says here comes from his own police statement." Never let a perpetrator's self-account become the narrator's voice without that frame.
  - Source: [Malcolm, "The Journalist and the Murderer", The New Yorker, 13 March 1989, opening](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1989/03/13/the-journalist-and-the-murderer-i)
- **Selection is your verdict** - Truman Capote kept himself out of In Cold Blood and still argued one view of the killer's motive: "I make my own comment by what I choose to tell." He chose that view and left out the rival ones.
  - In a narration script: the narrator never says "I believe", so every chosen detail argues. Write in the bible which version of motive or events the script adopts and which it drops; where a credible rival account exists, give it a sentence (see Leave room for two truths).
  - Source: [Plimpton, "The Story Behind a Nonfiction Novel", The New York Times, 16 January 1966](https://archive.nytimes.com/www.nytimes.com/books/97/12/28/home/capote-interview.html)
- **Tell two stories: the event and the belief** - Errol Morris: The Thin Blue Line shows what the truth most likely was and how people reached conclusions so far from it. Witnesses told stories of what they wished to see: "believing is seeing."
  - In a narration script: alternate beats between what happened and what investigators, witnesses or the jury believed at that point. The "but" in each hand-off is usually a piece of evidence that breaks a belief: "The cell records were the bedrock of the state's case. But read call by call, they did not support the witness's timeline."
  - Source: [Morris, Transom interview (2003), "Believing Is Seeing"](https://transom.org/2003/errol-morris-conversation/)
- **Keep one picky detail, leave the file in the bible** - Errol Morris: investigation and storytelling pull in opposite directions; a story simplifies, and its "absurd, picky little details" tie it to the world. He says almost all of his two years of investigation never reached the film.
  - In a narration script: give each chapter one verified, oddly specific detail (a stolen pair of monogrammed cuff links, a parking ticket) and move the rest of the research into the bible. Ten facts per chapter blur; one strange true one sticks.
  - Source: [Morris, Transom interview (2003), on investigation and storytelling](https://transom.org/2003/errol-morris-conversation/)
- **Take the viewer down the wrong turns, if you can back them up** - Sarah Koenig and Julie Snyder (Serial) give listeners the same base of knowledge "so that when we figure stuff out", everyone gets there together, and keep dead ends in "as long as we can corroborate what we're doing."
  - In a narration script: a sourced dead end can be a chapter's turn. Michelle McNamara's 2013 article spends a scene on cuff links she bought online as a possible clue; the detective later found they were not the same pair. State the lead, why it looked right, and what ruled it out. An unsourced theory never gets a chapter.
  - Sources: [Koenig and Snyder, Nieman Storyboard (2014)](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2014/10/30/serial-podcast-producers-talk-storytelling-structure-and-if-they-know-whodunnit/); [McNamara, "In the Footsteps of a Killer", Los Angeles magazine (2013), parts 1 and 5](https://lamag.com/crimeinla/in-the-footsteps-of-a-killer/)
- **Open on how they lived** - The Dart Center style guide asks reporters to "emphasize how they lived rather than how they died." Michelle McNamara noted that police files record the ordinariness of victims' lives in the moments before an attack.
  - In a narration script: the hook is an ordinary, documented moment in the victim's life, or the investigation's first strange fact, never the injuries. Suspense comes from what the viewer already knows and the person does not.
  - Sources: [Dart Center Style Guide, "Mass Shootings"](https://dartcenter.org/resources/dart-center-style-guide-trauma-informed-journalism); [McNamara (2013), part 9](https://lamag.com/crimeinla/in-the-footsteps-of-a-killer/)
- **Spend the offender's name carefully** - Michelle McNamara: "marketing matters"; a catchy name keeps a killer in the public mind, and she coined "Golden State Killer" herself. The Dart Center describes victims' families' "No Notoriety" requests and a middle path: use the name where the legal story needs it, not in headlines.
  - In a narration script: name victims early and in full (unless the bible says otherwise). Name the offender where identification or the trial needs it, and keep the name out of the title and the hook. Do not coin a nickname.
  - Sources: [McNamara (2013), part 4](https://lamag.com/crimeinla/in-the-footsteps-of-a-killer/); [Dart Center Style Guide, "Mass Shootings"](https://dartcenter.org/resources/dart-center-style-guide-trauma-informed-journalism)
- **Explain why it worked, not how to do it** - BBC Editorial Guidelines 8.4.5: "Criminal techniques should not normally be demonstrated or depicted in detail" because of imitation; 8.4.2 and 8.4.16 add that crime must not be glamorised.
  - In a narration script: for scams and heists, the story is the trust or the blind spot the scheme used, and what it cost the people on the other end. "The letters worked because each one named the reader's own bank." The tools, codes and sequence stay out.
  - Source: [BBC Editorial Guidelines, Section 8.4](https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/crime-and-anti-social-behaviour/guidelines)
- **Say who says so** - newscript.com's radio newswriting tutorial: rather than "alleged", "have your scripts reveal who is making the claim", always with the neutral verb "say". Reuters: "charges should not be conveyed as a sign of guilt."
  - In a narration script: until a conviction, every claim of guilt names its source. "Prosecutors said he drove the car" in place of "He drove the car." Use "accused" once per chapter at most, never "killer" or "murderer" before a verdict, and say "the jury found" for the verdict itself.
  - Sources: [newscript.com, "Charges & Allegations"](http://newscript.com/allege.html); [Reuters, "Reuters Journalistic Standards"](https://reutersagency.com/about/standards-values/)
- **Minimize harm to the people still living** - SPJ Code of Ethics: "Avoid pandering to lurid curiosity, even if others do"; private people control more of their own information than public figures. BBC 8.4.8-8.4.9 and 8.4.36: no guilt by association for relatives, care with people who changed names, and no identification by stacked details.
  - In a narration script: say what the violence was in one plain clause and move on. Relatives of the accused appear only for what they did, never as a shadow of the crime. A survivor's town, job and age together can identify her: keep only what the story needs.
  - Sources: [SPJ Code of Ethics (2014), "Minimize Harm"](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/); [BBC Editorial Guidelines, 8.4.8, 8.4.9, 8.4.36](https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/crime-and-anti-social-behaviour/guidelines)
- **Mark the reconstruction** - SPJ: "Clearly label illustrations and re-enactments." BBC 8.4.11 asks that crime reconstructions be made clear. Errol Morris: "Truth is not guaranteed by style or presentation."
  - In a narration script: every chapter's picture is a reconstruction, so the narration marks what the picture cannot: "Two witnesses described the car. This is the first description." When accounts conflict, say whose version the chapter shows before it shows it.
  - Sources: [SPJ Code of Ethics, "Seek Truth and Report It"](https://www.spj.org/spj-code-of-ethics/); [BBC Editorial Guidelines, 8.4.11](https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/crime-and-anti-social-behaviour/guidelines); [Morris, Transom interview (2003)](https://transom.org/2003/errol-morris-conversation/)
- **Real people, with room to breathe** - Julie Snyder (Serial) wanted listeners to understand "everybody is a real person" and not to "play it for exploitation". Koenig kept moments of levity that arose from the record, not "Insert joke here", and Snyder called them breathing room after a dense episode.
  - In a narration script: one light, true detail is allowed after a heavy chapter, at no one's expense among the victims or the bereaved. Never a joke placed to reset the mood.
  - Source: [Koenig and Snyder, Nieman Storyboard (2014)](https://niemanstoryboard.org/2014/10/30/serial-podcast-producers-talk-storytelling-structure-and-if-they-know-whodunnit/)
- **Let the evidence write the ending** - Sarah Koenig on the end of Serial's first season: "I can't pre-engineer this." She separated what she would decide as a juror from what she believed as a person. The Dart Center style guide warns against calling an outcome "closure" unless survivors say it.
  - In a narration script: end on the exact state of the evidence: what was proven, by whom, and what is still open. "The court overturned the conviction. No one has been charged since." Words like closure, healing or justice appear only inside a quote from the family.
  - Sources: [Koenig on Fresh Air, NPR transcript (23 December 2014)](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/372577482); [Dart Center Style Guide, "Closure"](https://dartcenter.org/resources/dart-center-style-guide-trauma-informed-journalism)

## Overrides to craft_principles.md
- Lead with the fact that sounds untrue: keep, but the fact comes from the record and is never an injury, a body or a lurid detail.
- Know the punchline before the first line: change - know the evidentiary ending (what is proven and what is not) before ch01. For an open case, the punchline is the precise doubt, not a solution.
- Pay off the hook in its own terms, then stop: keep, so a hook about an unsolved case must not promise a culprit. "Who did it" may only be the hook's question if the video names someone a court convicted.
- Leave room for two truths: tighten from advice to rule for contested convictions and unsolved cases. BBC 8.4.13: report both the prosecution and the defence, and report the verdict.
- Coincidence may cause trouble, never solve it: does not apply to real events. Cases are sometimes solved by chance; tell it as it happened and say it was chance.
- Suspense over surprise: keep for the investigation clock or a scam's mark, never by lingering on the minutes before a real person's death.
- Make me care, through people: keep; the person is the victim, the bereaved or the wrongly accused, drawn from how they lived. Someone the bible anonymises gets a role ("a neighbor"), never an invented name.
- A stand-in who knows as little as the viewer: keep; the detective, reporter or juror who learned the case step by step is the natural stand-in.
- Re-engage around minute three, then climb: keep, but the stairs are evidence and stakes, not escalating violence.
- Begin with the wrong idea: keep; the wrong idea is often the original verdict or the town's belief, stated fairly before the evidence against it.
- Intention and obstacle: keep; the agent with a goal is the investigator, the accused fighting a verdict, or the con artist, and the obstacle is evidence, time or the law. A victim is never reduced to an obstacle in someone else's plan.
- Numbers the ear can hold: keep, and treat clock times and dates the same way. Alibi cases live on times; give one time per sentence and anchor it ("at seven fifteen, twenty minutes after school let out").
- Add to the picture: tighten; the narration supplies the source and the status of what the viewer sees (record, testimony, or one witness's version), because the picture alone looks like fact.

## Bible additions
- Sources table: each factual claim in script.en.md mapped to a court record, police statement, trial transcript, official report or named published reporting, with date. Claims without a source are cut.
- Legal status of every named person: convicted, charged, arrested, suspect never charged, acquitted, conviction overturned, witness, victim; with the date last checked. This decides "said", "accused" and "convicted" in the script.
- Naming decisions: who is named in full, who is anonymised and by what role, minors, survivors of sexual violence, people who changed names (BBC 8.4.9), and the offender-name policy (Spend the offender's name carefully).
- Versions: where accounts conflict, each version with its source, and which version each chapter's reconstruction shows.
- Content notes per chapter: what violence is referred to; visual limits for the film (no act of violence, no bodies, injuries or crime-scene gore; places, objects, documents and timelines instead); a content warning line for the video description when needed.
- Method exclusions (scams, heists, poisonings, break-ins): the steps the narration and visuals leave out.
- Family contact: whether victims' families were informed or objected, if known (BBC 8.4.7).
- Live-case check: whether charges, a trial or an appeal are pending in any jurisdiction. If so, the script reports only the public record and adds no new claims about the evidence (BBC 8.4.13, 8.4.33).

## QA overrides
| Rule (narration-profile.md) | Change for this genre | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Invented specifics (P1) | P0 when the invented name, quote, act, motive or thought is attributed to a real person; P1 otherwise | Defamation and harm to living people, not just a craft slip |
| Vague attribution (strict) | Tighter: any statement of guilt, motive or wrongdoing about an unconvicted person without a named source ("police said", "the jury found") is P0 | Presumption of innocence; "alleged" alone does not protect |
| Rhetorical questions (one per chapter) | A question that insinuates guilt of a named person who was never convicted ("Was it the husband?") is P0. A question quoted from the record (a detective's or juror's) does not count toward the one-per-chapter limit | Insinuation is an accusation; quoted questions are evidence |
| Speculative openers / direct address | Second person that puts the viewer in the victim's place during the crime ("You wake to a flashlight in your eyes") is P1 | Turns a real person's assault into the viewer's thrill |
| Subjectless fragments | Strict: a fragment run like "No witnesses. No weapon. No motive." is P1 even at two fragments | The genre's most common stock rhythm |
| CTA | P1 if it asks viewers to guess or name a culprit, or if it follows the victim's death or a family's grief within the same chapter beat | Engagement bait that invites accusations against real people |
| Significance inflation | Strict, with genre forms: "one of the most shocking cases in history", "a case that gripped the nation" | Unverifiable superlatives about real tragedies |

Genre clichés to flag as P1: "in the wrong place at the wrong time", "little did they know", "a quiet town that would never be the same", "a crime that shocked the nation", "brutally murdered", "pure evil", "a monster", "senseless tragedy", "sent shockwaves through", "the truth was far more sinister", "but the killer made one mistake", "a web of lies", "justice was finally served", "finally found closure", "to this day, the case remains unsolved"
