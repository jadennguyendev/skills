# Fiction

Use when: the video tells an invented or traditional story through one narrator: drama, sci-fi, fantasy, fable, folk tale, retold myth, mystery fiction, romance. Combines with: horror (this file gives structure and voice, horror adds dread and its own QA rules), comedy (a comic tale: structure from here, timing from comedy), history-documentary (historical fiction: invented people in a real setting, every period fact still verified), explainer (a parable inside an explainer: the story runs on this file, the explanation around it on the core). Never with true-crime: a dramatized real case is true-crime.

The viewer should stop noticing that someone is talking and feel a thing happening to a person, then leave with an understanding no statement would have given them.
The core file is built for explanation, where verified fact and clarity lead. Fiction trades verification for coherence (the story's own canon and rules) and trades stated meaning for meaning carried by people and objects.

## Principles

- **Story is change; conflict is one engine of many** - Ursula K. Le Guin. Conflict "is one kind of behavior"; others are relating, finding, losing, bearing, discovering, parting, changing. Story is something or somebody changing. In "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" she calls the reduction of narrative to conflict absurd.
  - In a narration script: write the X/Y line as a change: who is different at the end, and what changed them. A ferryman who learns to leave his river is a story with no villain. If the outline only works once a fight is bolted on, the change is not found yet.
  - Source: [Le Guin, Steering the Craft (2015), p. 123, quoted on her site](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/bvc-navigating-the-ocean-of-story-session-2-round-1); [Le Guin, "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction" (1986), in Dancing at the Edge of the World](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/dancing-at-the-edge-of-the-world)
- **Tell the story, not the message** - Flannery O'Connor: "You tell a story because a statement would be inadequate"; a theme you can lift out of the story marks a weak story. Chekhov to Suvorin on his horse thieves: let the jury judge them, his job is to show what sort of people they are. Le Guin: "I don't speak message. I speak story."
  - In a narration script: no closing line that names the moral or theme. In a fable the last action carries it: "The fox walked past the vines and did not look up." If the channel wants a spoken moral, it is one plain line in the tale's own objects, after the action.
  - Source: [O'Connor, "Writing Short Stories", Mystery and Manners (1969)](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374508043/mysteryandmanners); [Chekhov to A. S. Suvorin, 1 April 1890, tr. Garnett](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6408); [Le Guin, "A Message About Messages"](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/message-about-messages)
- **The world's rules never change mid-story** - Le Guin to Alexei Mutovkin: coherence is the touchstone of plausibility, and the rules of the imagined world "cannot be changed during the story". Exact, sensory detail makes a world built of words plausible; passing mentions of places and events beyond the story make it feel larger. In "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" she warns that a writer can use all the trappings of fantasy "without ever actually imagining anything".
  - In a narration script: write the rules into the story bible (what the magic costs, how the ship travels, what the curse forbids) and resolve the climax only through a rule the viewer met earlier. Name one or two places or old events the story never visits; it costs a clause.
  - Source: [Le Guin, "Plausibility in Fantasy: an open letter" (2005)](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/plausibility-in-fantasy-an-open-letter); [Le Guin, "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" (1973), in The Language of the Night](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/the-language-of-the-night)
- **Crowd the scene, leap the gap** - Le Guin, "Crowding and Leaping": a story needs a focus and a trajectory more than a plot. Everything crowded in should sit inside the focus; every leap should follow the trajectory, the shape of the story's movement.
  - In a narration script: each chapter is one crowded scene, two at most, and the hard cut between chapters is the leap. Skip travel, sleep and "three days later" bridges; open the next chapter inside the next scene and place it in time with one clause: "By the third winter the well was dry."
  - Source: [Le Guin, Steering the Craft (2015), ch. 10 "Crowding and Leaping"](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/steering-the-craft)
- **Time takes no time; let events rhyme** - Italo Calvino on folktales: their first trait is "economy of expression", and everything mentioned has a function in the plot. Sicilian tellers say time takes no time in a story when they skip months or years. Oral telling leaves out needless detail but stresses repetition: in prose "there are events that rhyme."
  - In a narration script: build folk tales and fables as rhyming trials: the same test three times, the same phrase at each threshold, the third time broken. The repeat tells the viewer where they are after a hard cut. Cut any detail with no job, the way the ogre tale never says what illness the king had.
  - Source: [Calvino, Six Memos for the Next Millennium (1988), "Quickness"](https://books.google.com/books?id=vXCrX-B2AKUC)
- **One object that gathers meaning** - Flannery O'Connor: in good fiction some details "accumulate meaning from the action"; the wooden leg in "Good Country People" is "a wooden leg first". Calvino: an object that appears in a narrative is charged with force, like the pole of a magnetic field.
  - In a narration script: make the visual through-line a physical object, named plainly in ch01 and handled in every chapter: given, lost, broken, returned. Never say what it stands for. "She kept the ferry token in her shoe" works; "the token, a symbol of all she had lost" does not.
  - Source: [O'Connor, "Writing Short Stories", Mystery and Manners](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9780374508043/mysteryandmanners); [Calvino, "Quickness"](https://books.google.com/books?id=vXCrX-B2AKUC)
- **Open on a person, keep the lore for later** - Elmore Leonard: never open with weather unless it is a character's reaction to it, because the reader will "leaf ahead looking for people". A prologue is backstory, and "you can drop it in anywhere you want."
  - In a narration script: the first sentence of ch01 has a named person doing something under pressure: "Ana counted the coins twice before she knocked." Lore (the war, the empire, how the engine works) arrives one clause at a time when a character needs it, never as an opening paragraph.
  - Source: [Leonard, "Easy on the Adverbs, Exclamation Points and Especially Hooptedoodle", NYT (16 July 2001), rules 1-2](https://www.nytimes.com/2001/07/16/arts/writers-writing-easy-adverbs-exclamation-points-especially-hooptedoodle.html); [reprinted in Guardian, "Ten rules for writing fiction" (2010)](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2010/feb/20/ten-rules-for-writing-fiction-part-one)
- **Keep pins in the air** - George Saunders: a story makes meaning "a small structural pulse at a time". Each line raises expectations, and the reader is pleased when the story takes them into account, but not when it answers them too neatly. The opening throws bowling pins; the rest of the story catches them.
  - In a narration script: after each chapter, note in the chapter's `action` elements what the viewer now knows and expects. The next chapter catches at least one pin, a little off the line the viewer predicted. A pin still in the air at the last chapter is caught or cut from ch01.
  - Source: [Saunders, A Swim in a Pond in the Rain (2021), "A Page at a Time" (publisher excerpt)](https://www.bookreporter.com/reviews/a-swim-in-a-pond-in-the-rain/excerpt); [Saunders, "What writers really do when they write", Guardian (2017), part 7](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2017/mar/04/what-writers-really-do-when-they-write)
- **Something in relentless motion** - Raymond Carver: "There has to be tension, a sense that something is imminent"; a little menace is fine in a story. Part of the tension lives in what is left out and implied under the surface.
  - In a narration script: give every chapter one clock or threat that the narration names once and then leaves alone: the tide, the unopened letter, the footsteps upstairs. Mystery fiction runs on this: the viewer feels the answer coming before any clue is explained.
  - Source: [Carver, "A Storyteller's Shoptalk", New York Times Book Review (15 Feb 1981)](https://www.nytimes.com/1981/02/15/books/a-storyteller-s-shoptalk.html)
- **Pick the teller and hold the viewpoint** - Le Guin, "Point of View and Voice" and "Changing Point of View": she sets out first person, limited third person, involved author, detached author and observer-narrator, and treats a shift in viewpoint as a deliberate move. The Moth, for first-person tellers: state your fears, desires or dilemma, and "make us worry for you."
  - In a narration script: choose the teller in the outline. One voice suits first person (a character telling their own story) or the involved author (the folk-tale teller who knows everyone's heart). Limited third keeps a mystery fair: the viewer knows what the detective knows. Switch viewpoint only at a chapter cut, and only when the bible says so.
  - Source: [Le Guin, Steering the Craft (2015), ch. 7-8](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/steering-the-craft); [The Moth, "Storytelling Tips & Tricks"](https://www.themoth.org/tell-your-story/storytelling-tips-tricks)
- **Show the state through the act** - Anton Chekhov to his brother Alexander: avoid describing a character's state of mind; make it clear from what they do. For nature, seize small particulars: a moonlit night is a shard of broken bottle flashing on a mill dam.
  - In a narration script: write what the person does and let the picture show the face. "He set the cup down without drinking" replaces "fear washed over him". One exact detail per setting, the kind the eye would miss in the frame.
  - Source: [Chekhov to Al. P. Chekhov, 10 May 1886, Complete Works in 30 vols., Letters vol. 1 (Nauka, 1974), pp. 241-243](https://chehov-lit.ru/chehov/letters/1875-1886/letter-176.htm)
- **Dialogue in one voice: "said", or no tag** - Elmore Leonard: never carry dialogue with a verb other than "said", never put an adverb on it, use dialect sparingly. Chekhov, same 1886 letter: don't chase a crowd of characters; the center of gravity should be two, he and she.
  - In a narration script: the narrator voices most lines (the tts skill gives only a few climax lines their own character voice), so the tag goes before the line and then gets out of the way: Mira said, "Leave the lamp." Keep exchanges to two speakers and about four lines; drop the tag when the words name the speaker. No phonetic dialect, which TTS mangles.
  - Source: [Leonard, NYT (2001), rules 3, 4, 7](https://www.nytimes.com/2001/07/16/arts/writers-writing-easy-adverbs-exclamation-points-especially-hooptedoodle.html); [Chekhov, 10 May 1886](https://chehov-lit.ru/chehov/letters/1875-1886/letter-176.htm)
- **At the worst moment, write colder** - Chekhov to Lydia Avilova: weep over your stories if you must, but so the reader does not notice: "The more objective, the stronger will be the effect." Le Guin: pity and terror come more often "through brevity, a few telling details, a great left unsaid".
  - In a narration script: the climax and any death or violence get short complete sentences and the fewest adjectives in the script. One concrete detail, then the cut. The picture and score carry the feeling; the narration says what happened.
  - Source: [Chekhov to L. A. Avilova, 29 April 1892, tr. Garnett](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6408); [Le Guin, Book View Café answer 5 (2016)](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/bvc-navigating-the-ocean-of-story-session-2-round-1)
- **No tricks: the end fulfils the beginning** - Raymond Carver: "No tricks." Le Guin: think of a story as a whole in which the beginning implies the end and the end fulfils the beginning. Saunders: the best pins come down in a surprising way that carries more meaning than the writer planned.
  - In a narration script: a twist makes earlier chapters read truer, using only what the viewer already saw; it never cancels them. Replay ch01 after the twist: every line must still be honest. The last line returns to ch01's object or place, changed.
  - Source: [Carver, "A Storyteller's Shoptalk" (1981)](https://www.nytimes.com/1981/02/15/books/a-storyteller-s-shoptalk.html); [Le Guin, Book View Café answer 2 (2016)](https://www.ursulakleguin.com/bvc-navigating-the-ocean-of-story-session-2-round-1)

## Overrides to craft_principles.md

- Solve for Y: keep, but Y is a change in a person or a fresh angle on a known tale (the wolf's side of it), not a surprising fact.
- No surprise, no video: change - a retold folk tale can be known to everyone; the surprise lives in the telling and the turn. Glass's test still holds for invented plots: if it reminds you of other stories, rework it.
- Lead with the fact that sounds untrue: does not apply. Herzog's licence to fabricate applies in full, inside the story bible.
- Leave room for two truths: keep - give the antagonist a reason the viewer can follow.
- Intention and obstacle; Say what happens if they fail: keep, widened - the obstacle can be a loss, a parting or a silence, not only an opponent.
- Deliver the packaging in the first 30 seconds: keep - within the first 75 words: the person, the place, and the trouble the title promises.
- Re-engage around minute three: keep - put the first reversal there.
- Section 5 (explanation and clarity): does not apply, except "Convert exposition to ammunition", which governs all world lore.
- Add to the picture: keep, stricter - narration never describes what the `action` elements already show.
- Action, then what it means: change - meaning arrives through a character's act or words, never a narrator's reflection (see "Tell the story, not the message").
- Pay off the hook in its own terms, then stop: keep; a folk tale may add one closing formula after the payoff.
- Revision checklist: items 9-11 do not apply. Item 1 becomes "Who changes, and what changed them?". Add: every rule used in the climax appears earlier; every chapter catches a pin an earlier chapter threw.

## Bible additions

- Story bible in the bible's `series`: cast (name, age, a one-line look kept identical across chapters, what they want, what they fear), places, world rules (costs and limits of magic or technology), and the through-line object with its state at the end of each chapter.
- Point of view and tense, fixed for the video; any planned viewpoint switch named with its chapter.
- Per chapter: "Viewer must feel" next to "Viewer must understand"; "Pins thrown / pins caught"; story time and place, so the picture lands the leap after each hard cut.
- Canon list inside the story bible, in place of `facts_to_verify`: every name, number, date and object state the narration uses, so script-qa can check invented specifics. Real-world facts inside the fiction (a historical setting, the physics of hard sci-fi) still go under `facts_to_verify`.
- Origin for retellings: collection, teller or tale type, and whether the source text is public domain. Adapting copyrighted fiction needs the rights holder's permission; flag it to the user before outlining.
- Content notes: violence, death, self-harm, harm to children or animals, and how the script handles each (offscreen, one line), for YouTube age and advertiser rules.
- Voices: which character lines, if any, get their own voice, noted with the character in the bible; the first line of such a character is still tagged.

## QA overrides

| Rule (narration-profile.md) | Change for this genre | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Invented specifics (P1) | Relax, per the fiction definition in narration-profile.md: a contradiction of the story bible's canon list, or a new named character, place or rule missing from it, stays P1 | Fiction invents by design; a continuity error is the real fault |
| Speculative openers | Keep strict; "In a world where" and an opening of lore with no person in it are P1 too | The stock fiction opener |
| Formula openers and closers | Relax for folk tale and fable only: one opening formula ("Once there was a miller...") and one closing formula; the next sentence puts a person in action | A traditional oral device, not an AI tell |
| Rhetorical questions | Questions inside a character's line do not count; narrator questions to the viewer stay at one per chapter | Dialogue is not narrator address |
| Direct address ("you", "we") | Tighten: narrator address to the viewer inside the story is P2; second-person telling only when the bible names it, then held throughout | Address breaks the spell |
| "Let's" constructions | Tighten: none inside the story | Same |
| Subjectless fragments | Keep: three or more in a row is P1, the climax included | Short complete sentences carry a climax; fragment runs are the tell |
| Dialogue tags (new, P2) | A tag verb other than "said" (or "asked" for a question), an adverb on a tag, or phonetic dialect | Leonard; TTS reads dialect badly |
| Stated moral or theme (new, P2) | A line that names the lesson, scored as performed insight | O'Connor and Le Guin |
| CTA | Tighten: after the story's last line, in the narrator's plain voice; never inside a scene, never explaining the ending | A CTA inside the story breaks the ending |
| Em dashes | Strict in dialogue too: an interrupted line ends with a period | TTS reads dashes inconsistently |

Genre clichés to flag as P1: "little did she know", "a chill ran down her spine", "suddenly", "all hell broke loose", "let out a breath she didn't know she was holding", "time seemed to stand still", "in a world where", "it was a dark and stormy night", "the silence was deafening", "every fiber of her being", "a smile tugged at the corner of her lips", "eyes that held a thousand stories", "nothing would ever be the same", "it was all just a dream", "and so the journey began"
