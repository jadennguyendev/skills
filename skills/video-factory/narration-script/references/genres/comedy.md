# Comedy

Use when: the user asks for a funny, satirical or parody video, a comedic explainer, a sketch or bit told by one narrator, a roast of an idea, product or institution, or any script with the `humorous` tone. Combines with: explainer (comedic explainer: the core file carries the facts and causality, this file carries the voice and the take; every fact stays verified), history-documentary (comic history: the record is told straight and the joke is how strange it already is), fiction (a sketch or comic story: the invented world is the premise and the take is its rule), horror (relief beats in early chapters only, per horror.md; Twain's pause works for both), true-crime (rarely: jokes never land on victims or survivors).

The viewer should laugh at things they catch a beat before the narrator seems to, and leave holding the video's opinion without feeling lectured. The core craft explains and then reflects; comedy plays the explanation straight, drops the joke without pointing at it, and lets the viewer do the last step.

## Principles

- **Start from an opinion, not a joke** - Scott Dikkers (The Onion). Every piece has a subtext, and subtext is "just an opinion or a value judgment", rarely funny on its own. He starts writers by listing ten opinions and filtering one through a comic device such as irony or exaggeration.
  - In a narration script: under the X/Y line, write the opinion the video argues in plain words ("Meetings exist mostly to schedule more meetings"). If no opinion fits, the video has a topic and no comedy yet.
  - Source: [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 09:52-11:42 and 13:10-15:32 (2026)](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **One take, one track, escalated** - Scott Dikkers. "In short-form comedy, the take is everything": pick the one obsessive idea, stay strictly on it, escalate it to absurdity, then stop. A joke off the track makes the piece wobble.
  - In a narration script: state the take in one sentence in the bible. Each chapter is the next rung of the same idea, larger than the last (one meeting, the meeting about that meeting, the committee that schedules the committee). Cut jokes that are funny but off the track.
  - Source: [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 06:14-07:52](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **If this is true, what else is true?** - Matt Besser, Ian Roberts and Matt Walsh (UCB). Heightening answers that question with a move in keeping with, yet more absurd than, everything before it; random escalation does not heighten. Exploring asks "then why is it true?" and supplies the logic behind the absurd behavior.
  - In a narration script: write each chapter's rung as an answer to "what else is true". Exploring is where a comedic explainer's verified facts go; in a sketch the reason may be invented and logged: "The rule exists because in 1974 one man lost one stapler."
  - Source: [Besser, Roberts and Walsh, The Upright Citizens Brigade Comedy Improvisation Manual (2013), ch. 5 "Playing Game", p. 113](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL17346764W)
- **Agree with the premise and add to it** - Tina Fey. Improv's first rules: agree and say yes, then "YES, AND": accept what has been created and add something of your own. A third rule, make statements, swaps questions for claims.
  - In a narration script: once the premise is set, the narrator never undercuts it ("obviously this is ridiculous"). Each line accepts the last and adds a fact or consequence, stated flatly: "The committee approved the meeting. It then scheduled a second meeting to discuss the first."
  - Source: [Fey, Bossypants (Reagan Arthur Books, 2011), the rules of improvisation](https://archive.org/details/bossypants0000feyt_u3v6)
- **Broad on top, deep underneath** - Scott Dikkers. His rule for The Onion's first book: the bigger the headline, the more accessible the joke must be, so everyone gets it. Deep in the piece, an obscure reference rewards the few who catch it.
  - In a narration script: the title, thumbnail and first chapter carry a joke any viewer gets. Niche references and in-jokes go to the middle chapters, where missing one costs nothing.
  - Source: [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 16:02-18:47](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **Map a hidden analogy** - Scott Dikkers. Keep one thing unmentioned and map its tropes onto the thing you are talking about; each time the viewer recognizes the hidden thing, that is a joke beat. His example: Seinfeld's switch to a new barber, played beat for beat as an affair.
  - In a narration script: pick the hidden frame in the outline (a software update told as a breakup, a tax form as a hostage letter) and never name it. Every chapter maps a new trope of it onto the topic, which also gives the chapters one through-line.
  - Source: [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 34:46-37:13](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **Funny from the first line, funnier by the end** - Jerry Seinfeld and Scott Dikkers. Seinfeld likes "the first line to be funny right away", and in a long bit the biggest laugh has to come at the end. Dikkers rejected the old build-to-a-punchline story: funny from the start, only getting funnier.
  - In a narration script: ch01's first sentence already carries the take as a joke, inside the first three seconds. Within each chapter, order beats from smallest laugh to largest and put the largest last, right before the hand-off.
  - Sources: [Seinfeld, "How to Write a Joke", The New York Times (2012)](https://www.nytimes.com/video/magazine/100000001965963/jerry-seinfeld-how-to-write-a-joke-.html); [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 25:34-27:15](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **The straight voice carries the joke** - Mark Twain and Scott Dikkers. Twain: the humorous story "is told gravely", and the teller hides that he suspects anything is funny. Dikkers: the ideal Onion story has no visible jokes, strict news style around an absurd premise, often with the joke as a paragraph's last word.
  - In a narration script: the narrator is the straight person, calm and exact, and the absurdity sits in the content. "The report runs four hundred pages. Page one recommends a shorter report." Give the TTS voice a dry, unhurried instruction; a grinning read gives the joke away.
  - Sources: [Twain, "How to Tell a Story" (1895)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3250/3250-h/3250-h.htm); [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 29:39-31:54 and 41:52-44:02](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **Slur the nub, never point at it** - Mark Twain and Steve Martin. Twain's master drops the point "in a carefully casual and indifferent way"; the comic teller shouts it, italicizes it and explains it. Martin saw an audience laugh at a comic's hand-slap cue when the punch line itself was unintelligible, and built an act with no indicators.
  - In a narration script: no cue words before a joke ("Funnily enough", "Get this", "And the best part") and no explanation after it. If a line only works with a cue, rewrite the line. E. B. White's frog applies: a dissected joke dies.
  - Sources: [Twain, "How to Tell a Story"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3250/3250-h/3250-h.htm); [Martin, "Being Funny", Smithsonian (February 2008), adapted from Born Standing Up](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/being-funny-17061140/); [White, preface, A Subtreasury of American Humor (1941), p. xvii](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.260442)
- **The pause goes before the snapper** - Mark Twain. The pause must be "exactly the right length": too short and the point is missed, too long and the audience guesses a surprise is coming. Artemus Ward let his animation die, paused, then added the incongruous remark as if to himself.
  - In a narration script: TTS times pauses by punctuation, so the snapper gets its own short sentence after a full stop or a paragraph break, with no teaser in front. "He had no teeth at all. He was also the best drummer in the county." Log each snapper in the bible so the edit can leave room.
  - Source: [Twain, "How to Tell a Story"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3250/3250-h/3250-h.htm)
- **Fewest syllables, most funny words** - Jerry Seinfeld. He will "shave letters off of words" and count syllables to tighten the link between beats, calls it closer to songwriting, and praises one line because four of its seven words are funny. A specific detail (the back of his head, not his head) starts the bit.
  - In a narration script: cut every word between the setup and the snapper that is not a joke word or a load-bearing fact. Prefer the exact, odd detail ("the beige folder") to the general one ("the paperwork").
  - Source: [Seinfeld, "How to Write a Joke", The New York Times (2012)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=itWxXyCfW5s)
- **Commit; never show doubt** - Steve Martin. His rule: "Never let them know I was bombing." The act moves on without pausing for laughs, as if it would continue with or without the audience, and the laughs catch up.
  - In a narration script: no apologies, winks or laugh-begging ("okay, bad joke", "stay with me here"). The narrator moves to the next beat at full confidence, which also suits a recorded voice that cannot wait for laughter.
  - Source: [Martin, "Being Funny", Smithsonian (2008)](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/being-funny-17061140/)
- **Sympathy over contempt; hide the pill** - E. B. White and Scott Dikkers. White: "It is sympathy, not contempt or derision," that makes comic characters live, and humor "won't stand much blowing up". Dikkers: satire flops when it turns preachy; once the viewer can "taste the medicine", it has failed.
  - In a narration script: aim the joke at a behavior, a system or the narrator, and give its target one human reason for acting as it does. The opinion stays inside the jokes; a paragraph that argues it outright gets cut.
  - Sources: [White, preface, A Subtreasury of American Humor, pp. xv and xviii](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.260442); [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 46:06-49:12](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **Play the picture straight** - Scott Dikkers. The Onion rule: never run a funny photo, because "the photo takes all the heat"; run a straight one and put the joke in the angle on it.
  - In a narration script: the `action` elements ask for a sober, documentary-looking film and lets the narration supply the absurdity, or names one specific visual gag per chapter that the narration then leaves alone.
  - Source: [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 00:56-01:18](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)
- **End on a button that steps sideways** - Scott Dikkers. Every short comic piece needs a closing joke, and it should feel different from the escalation: take a sidestep, underplay, widen the world, or step back into the real one.
  - In a narration script: after the last rung of the take, the final line turns the angle once ("The committee has since been asked to review this video. They have scheduled a meeting."). Then stop; the core rule on endings still applies.
  - Source: [Dikkers on How I Write, transcript 61:40-64:04](https://howiwrite.substack.com/p/scott-dikkers-11-ways-to-make-your)

## Overrides to craft_principles.md

- Solve for Y: keep; Y is the take, and the opinion behind it goes on the next line of the outline.
- Therefore or but, never "and then": keep, as the escalation logic. Each rung follows from the last ("the meeting ran long, therefore it needed a follow-up").
- Suspense over surprise: keep for the long question only. At the joke level surprise wins: a telegraphed joke is dead (Twain on a pause held too long).
- Open the gap between expectation and result: keep and multiply; every joke is a small gap, several per chapter.
- Give two plus two, not four: tighten; the viewer adds up the joke, the narrator never says the sum.
- Begin with the wrong idea: keep for comedic explainers; the common belief, stated fairly and gravely, is often the best straight line in the chapter.
- Numbers the ear can hold: keep; an absurd number still gets rounded and compared, and the comparison is usually the joke.
- Add to the picture: tighten; the narration and the picture never deliver the same joke (see Play the picture straight).
- Fresh figures or none: change; comic comparisons are joke beats, so more than one per chapter is fine when each serves the take. Stock figures stay banned.
- Make me care, through people: change; a sketch may invent its characters, logged in the bible. Real people appear only with verified facts, and no invented quote is put in a real person's mouth.
- Action, then what it means: change; no reflection after a joke. A comedic explainer keeps one factual reflection per chapter, phrased straight and placed away from the snapper.
- Pay off the hook in its own terms, then stop: keep; the button is that payoff, and the CTA sits just before it (see QA overrides).
- Kill the weak parts whole: tighten; draft about three jokes for every one kept, as Onion writers pitch hundreds of headlines for a dozen stories.

## Bible additions

- **The take**: the opinion in one plain line, the take in one sentence, and the escalation ladder (one rung per chapter).
- **Target**: who or what each joke lands on. Private individuals and traits people cannot change are out; public figures and institutions are in only for what they did.
- **Real vs invented**: real people, companies and events with their verified facts in `facts_to_verify`; a separate "Comic inventions" list (invented characters, numbers, quotes, documents) so QA can tell a planted absurdity from an invented fact. An invention must be plainly impossible or clearly fictional in context.
- **Visual register**: "play it straight" by default, with any visual gag named per chapter. No on-screen laugh cues, reaction emojis or wacky sound effects unless the user asks.
- **Snappers**: the snapper line of each chapter, so the voice and the edit leave a beat of room before it.
- **Voice**: dry, unhurried, unamused; note it in the tts instruct.
- **Callback ledger**: each running gag, where it is set up and where it pays off.

## QA overrides

| Rule (narration-profile.md) | Change for this genre | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Invented specifics (P1) | relaxed for items on the bible's "Comic inventions" list; strict for anything said about a real person, company or event | A sketch's absurd number is the joke; a made-up fact about a real target is a falsehood |
| Manufactured punchlines, repeated setup/reversal (P2) | relaxed: reversals that escalate the take are allowed, several per chapter; still a finding when a reversal replaces a fact the explanation needed | patterns.md carves out intentional comedy; the tell is reversal standing in for content |
| Compulsive rule of three | relaxed: a triple whose third item is the joke passes, once per chapter; ornamental triads stay findings | patterns.md keeps triads that serve a deliberate rhetorical purpose |
| CTA placement | relaxed: the CTA may come just before the final line, so the video ends on the button | Ending on the laugh matters more than ending on the ask (Dikkers on the button) |
| Performed insight ("That is the whole trick", "the punchline is") | tightened to P1 | Announcing the joke is Twain's comic-story failure and the most common AI comedy move |
| Fake-casual props ("checks notes", "chef's kiss", "mic drop", "because of course it did") | tightened to P1 | These are the stock kit models reach for when told to be funny |
| Staccato drama (3+ fragments) | unchanged, P1 | A joke built from clipped fragments is a drumroll, and Twain's teller never drums |

Genre clichés to flag as P1: "Plot twist:", "Spoiler alert:", "Narrator: they did not", "What could possibly go wrong?", "Because of course it did", "said no one ever", "You can't make this stuff up", "checks notes", "chef's kiss", "mic drop", "Buckle up", "Let that sink in", "And that's when things got weird", "Hold my beer", "Ten out of ten, would recommend"
