# Craft Principles for Narration Scripts

Story and explanation principles from screenwriters, documentary makers, editors, radio producers and YouTube creators, each traced to the person's own book, talk, letter or document. This file complements `script_formats.md` (structures, templates, timing) and does not repeat it.
Every genre uses it; genre files in `genres/` add to it and override what does not fit. Read it before step 2 (outline) and again before step 3 (write); run the revision checklist at the end before handing the draft to script-qa.
Every example sentence here is meant to pass `../../script-qa/narration-profile.md`. Where a famous technique collides with that profile, the entry says how to use it without the tell.

## 1. Premise and angle

- **Solve for Y** - Alex Blumberg (This American Life producer). Pitch the story in one strict form: "I'm doing a story about X. And what's interesting about it is Y." If Y is what anyone would guess, you are on the wrong track; look for the corner of the topic that surprises.
  - In a narration script: write the X/Y line at the top of the outline. "A video about printing money, and what's interesting is that the bakery bakes ten loaves either way" works. "...and what's interesting is that inflation is complicated" does not.
  - Source: [Blumberg, Transom (2005)](https://transom.org/2005/alex-blumberg/)
- **Most economical telling first** - Emma Coats (Pixar story artist), story basic #22. Find the essence of the story and its most economical telling, then build out from there.
  - In a narration script: before the outline table, tell the whole video in two or three spoken sentences. If that fails, the outline will sprawl.
  - Source: [Coats, #storybasics 22 (2011)](https://x.com/lawnrocket/status/66556192868143104)
- **No surprise, no video** - Kurzgesagt's editorial team scraps a topic when the facts they find are not surprising or fresh enough. Ira Glass rules out stories that remind you of other stories or work out as you'd expect: "Surprise is important."
  - In a narration script: before outlining, list the three facts a viewer is least likely to know. If none would make a friend stop you to ask "really?", propose a different angle to the user instead of writing.
  - Sources: [Kurzgesagt, "How research and factchecking work at Kurzgesagt" (2023)](https://medium.com/@Kurzgesagt/how-research-and-factchecking-work-at-kurzgesagt-f5b239188255); [Glass, Transom manifesto part 2 (2004)](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/)
- **Lead with the fact that sounds untrue** - Werner Herzog, Minnesota Declaration: facts "sometimes have a strange and bizarre power" that makes their truth seem unbelievable.
  - In a narration script: build the hook or a turn on a verified fact of that kind ("In 1923 a loaf of bread in Berlin cost two hundred billion marks."). Herzog's licence to reach truth through "fabrication and imagination and stylization" does not transfer to explainers: every figure goes into the chapter's `facts_to_verify`.
  - Source: [Herzog, Minnesota Declaration, points 3 and 5 (1999)](https://www.wernerherzog.com/files/wernerherzog/docs/doc_text_minnesota.pdf)
- **Know the punchline before the first line** - Andrew Stanton (Pixar): storytelling is joke telling. Know your ending, so everything from the first sentence to the last leads to a singular goal.
  - In a narration script: write the last chapter's payoff sentence before ch01's hook, then check that the hook asks the question that sentence answers.
  - Source: [Stanton, "The clues to a great story", TED (2012)](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story)
- **Leave room for two truths** - Ken Burns: audiences default to a "binary one and zero dialectic", while in war "there can be more than one truth."
  - In a narration script: when a topic has two camps, the angle is often the case where both are partly right. Say what each side gets right and show the evidence for each.
  - Source: [Burns on Recode Media, full transcript, Vox (2017)](https://www.vox.com/2017/9/19/16334444/transcript-filmmaker-ken-burns-vietnam-war-film-documentary-recode-media)

## 2. Structure and causality

- **Therefore or but, never "and then"** - Trey Parker and Matt Stone. If "and then" belongs between the beats of your outline, the result is boring; between every beat there should be either the word "therefore" or "but". That causation is what makes it a story.
  - In a narration script: read the outline's hand-off column aloud joined by those words. "The council prints money, therefore every price on the bakery board doubles. But the baker still has ten loaves." A beat that only joins with "and then" gets rebuilt or cut.
  - Source: [Parker and Stone, mtvU "Stand In" at NYU, 0:48-2:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vGUNqq3jVLg)
- **Because, not after** - Aristotle, Poetics. Each event should follow from the last by necessity or probability; it matters whether an event is "propter hoc or post hoc". Surprise hits hardest when events also "follow as cause and effect."
  - In a narration script: chronology is the explainer's trap ("In 1971... Then in 1980... Then in 2008..." is a list of dates). Order the chapters by what caused what, and give a date only where the date changes the meaning.
  - Source: [Poetics, tr. S. H. Butcher, Parts IX-X](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.1.1.html)
- **Remove it and see** - Aristotle, Poetics Part VIII: a part whose presence or absence "makes no visible difference, is not an organic part of the whole."
  - In a narration script: for each chapter, ask whether the ending still lands without it. If it does, merge or cut the chapter; every chapter costs its own shots and screen time.
  - Source: [Poetics, tr. Butcher, Part VIII](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.1.1.html)
- **The story spine** - Emma Coats, story basic #4: once upon a time there was a situation, every day something held, one day something changed, because of that (twice), until finally.
  - In a narration script: fill it in for an explainer before the outline. "Every day, ten villagers trade coins for bread at steady prices. One day the council prints a billion. Because of that, everyone bids more. Because of that, the baker raises prices. Until finally, a billion buys the same ten loaves." Each slot becomes a chapter or a turn.
  - Source: [Coats, #storybasics 4 (2012)](https://x.com/lawnrocket/status/201018115604230146)
- **Coincidence may cause trouble, never solve it** - Emma Coats, story basic #19: coincidences that get characters into trouble are great; coincidences that get them out are cheating.
  - In a narration script: luck can open a problem ("A lab assistant left a dish uncovered over a weekend"), but the resolution must come from evidence or reasoning the viewer watched. A lucky break at the climax reads as a skipped step.
  - Source: [Coats, #storybasics 19 (2011)](https://x.com/lawnrocket/status/62943719158059008)
- **Open the gap between expectation and result** - Robert McKee: the substance of story is the gap between what a person expects to happen when they act and what does happen.
  - In a narration script: give each chapter one moment where the obvious prediction fails. State the expectation plainly first ("Twice the money should buy twice the bread."), then show the result.
  - Source: [McKee, Story (1997), "The Substance of Story"](https://archive.org/details/storysubstancest0000mcke)
- **Start close to the end; make every sentence work** - Kurt Vonnegut: every sentence must either reveal character or advance the action. "Start as close to the end as possible."
  - In a narration script: ch01 opens where the problem bites, not with the history of the field; background arrives when a question needs it. A sentence that neither moves the explanation forward nor shows a person is cut.
  - Source: [Vonnegut, Bagombo Snuff Box (1999), introduction, rules 4-5](https://archive.org/details/bagombosnuffboxu00vonn)

## 3. Hooks and openings

- **The opening is a promise** - Andrew Stanton: a good beginning promises that the story will lead somewhere worth your time; a well-told promise is "like a pebble being pulled back in a slingshot."
  - In a narration script: the hook states a concrete question or claim that the last chapter pays off. Stock openers fail because they promise nothing specific.
  - Source: [Stanton, TED (2012)](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story)
- **Deliver the packaging in the first 30 seconds** - MrBeast's production guide: the title and thumbnail set the viewer's expectations, and the first minute is where most viewers leave. YouTube's own intro metric counts who is still watching at 30 seconds and links a high score to an opening that matches the title and thumbnail.
  - In a narration script: ask for the planned title and thumbnail before writing ch01. The first ~75 words (30 s at 150 wpm) must show or name what they promise.
  - Sources: [How to Succeed in MrBeast Production, pp. 5-7](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6623bf84e83241ec49b548e4/66edaa19db6e9359bb92931f_How-To-Succeed-At-MrBeast-Production%20(2).pdf); [YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)
- **Bait that is legit** - Derek Muller (Veritasium) separates attention-seeking packaging, which is the job, from the kind that is sensationalized or misleading and withholds information to exploit the curiosity gap. Every video has many accurate framings that still entice.
  - In a narration script: a hook may hold back the answer but may not overstate the claim. Test it against the last chapter: if the viewer would feel the hook lied, or the claim needs "kind of" to stay true, rewrite it.
  - Source: [Muller, "Clickbait is Unreasonably Effective", 3:20-6:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S2xHZPH5Sng)

## 4. Tension, curiosity and retention

- **Suspense over surprise** - Alfred Hitchcock to François Truffaut: a bomb that goes off unannounced gives the audience a few seconds of surprise; show them the bomb first and "we have provided them with fifteen minutes of suspense."
  - In a narration script: tell the viewer early what is at risk and when it will be decided, then explain. "The loan comes due on the first of March. The company has nine weeks to find the money." Knowing the deadline is what makes the next chapters tense.
  - Source: [Truffaut, Hitchcock (1967), "An example of suspense"](https://archive.org/details/hitchcock00truf)
- **Short tension inside long tension** - Andrew Stanton, quoting playwright William Archer: drama is "anticipation mingled with uncertainty." Finding Nemo runs short tension (will Dory forget what she was told?) inside a global one (will they find Nemo?).
  - In a narration script: one long question for the whole video, set in ch01 and answered in the last chapter; one short question per chapter, answered inside it or at its hand-off. The through-line object carries the long question.
  - Source: [Stanton, TED (2012)](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story)
- **Give two plus two, not four** - Andrew Stanton: "Don't give them four, give them two plus two"; audiences want to work for their meal without noticing. Billy Wilder passes on Ernst Lubitsch's version: let the audience add up two plus two.
  - In a narration script: put two facts side by side and let the viewer reach the sum a beat before the narration does. "Everyone now holds twice as many coins. The bakery still holds ten loaves." The next line builds on the sum; announcing it ("That is the whole trick") is a flagged tell.
  - Sources: [Stanton, TED (2012)](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story); [Crowe, Conversations with Wilder (1999)](https://archive.org/details/conversationswit0000wild)
- **Withhold the answer, never the context** - Kurt Vonnegut: "Give your readers as much information as possible as soon as possible." Robert McKee: withhold everything except what comprehension needs, arouse curiosity by putting "Why?" in the viewer's mind, and save the critical fact for last.
  - In a narration script: the two agree once context and answer are split. Name the open question in plain words, give every fact needed to follow it, and hold back only the answer: "Why did bread cost more when nobody baked less? The baker's ledger shows why." A teaser that names nothing ("But here's where it gets interesting") withholds context, and that is the AI tell.
  - Sources: [Vonnegut, Bagombo Snuff Box, rule 8](https://archive.org/details/bagombosnuffboxu00vonn); [McKee, Story, ch. "Exposition", pp. 336-337](https://archive.org/details/storysubstancest0000mcke)
- **Re-engage around minute three, then climb** - MrBeast's guide plans a "3 minute re-engagement", puts the most exciting simple material in minutes 3-6, and uses "stair stepping": stakes rise each step to a payoff at the end. "Strong payoffs at the end of videos boost retention."
  - In a narration script: in a 10-minute video, place a striking verified fact or demonstration in ch02-ch03, not only in the hook. Order examples from smallest to largest (one family, one town, one country) so each chapter raises the stakes.
  - Source: [How to Succeed in MrBeast Production, pp. 8 onward](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6623bf84e83241ec49b548e4/66edaa19db6e9359bb92931f_How-To-Succeed-At-MrBeast-Production%20(2).pdf)
- **Do not promise what you will not deliver** - Anton Chekhov, reviewing a friend's play: cut a monologue that promises much to the audience but connects to nothing; a loaded rifle must not stand on stage if no one means to fire it. "One must not promise."
  - In a narration script: every hook question, named character and "remember this number" needs a payoff. In revision, pair each setup with the line that pays it off; cut setups with no payoff, and plant a setup earlier for any payoff that arrives from nowhere.
  - Source: [Chekhov, letter to A. S. Lazarev-Gruzinsky, 1 Nov 1889, Complete Works, Letters vol. 3, pp. 273-275 (Russian; translation ours)](https://chehov-lit.ru/chehov/letters/1888-1889/letter-707.htm)

## 5. Explanation and clarity (explainers and nonfiction)

- **Begin with the wrong idea** - Derek Muller's PhD research: a clear, correct physics video left test scores flat (6.0 to 6.3 out of 26) while viewers grew more confident; a video that voiced the common misconception and then refuted it nearly doubled scores (11 out of 26), though viewers called it confusing.
  - In a narration script: state the common belief fairly, in the words a viewer would use, show where it breaks, then give the correct idea. "Most people would guess the new money makes everyone richer, at least for a while. Watch the bakery."
  - Source: [Muller, "Khan Academy and the Effectiveness of Science Videos", 2:20-7:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eVtCO84MDj8)
- **Convert exposition to ammunition** - Robert McKee: dramatize exposition; pass on a fact only when its absence would cause confusion; least important facts early, critical ones last.
  - In a narration script: introduce a term at the moment the argument needs it, after the viewer has seen the thing it names. Show the bids rising first, then say "Economists call this inflation." A chapter of definitions up front loses viewers before the question exists.
  - Source: [McKee, Story, ch. "Exposition"](https://archive.org/details/storysubstancest0000mcke)
- **A stand-in who knows as little as the viewer** - Aaron Sorkin: courtroom drama makes exposition work because the jury "knows as little as the audience does", so the lawyers explain to both.
  - In a narration script: give the through-line a character who asks the viewer's question (a villager who has never heard of a central bank). The question earns the explanation and keeps it inside the story world.
  - Source: [Sorkin, WGA East OnWriting ep. 41, transcript (2021)](https://www.wgaeast.org/onwriting/aaron-sorkin-the-trial-of-the-chicago-7/)
- **Simplify on purpose, and say so once** - Kurzgesagt aims for every simplification to be "a deliberate choice"; their fact checkers ask whether a concept was simplified so far that it misleads.
  - In a narration script: log each simplification in the bible's `series.notes`. If one changes the answer for some viewers, add one plain sentence: "Real towns have more than one bakery, but the logic holds."
  - Source: [Kurzgesagt, "How research and factchecking work at Kurzgesagt" (2023)](https://medium.com/@Kurzgesagt/how-research-and-factchecking-work-at-kurzgesagt-f5b239188255)
- **Numbers the ear can hold** - NPR Training: numbers make listeners do math, and spoken numbers are easily missed, misheard or forgotten; the ear handles about one fact or idea per sentence.
  - In a narration script: one number per sentence, rounded, with a comparison the viewer can picture, and do the arithmetic for them: "State A spends about twice what State B does." Exact figures go on screen through the chapter's `action` elements.
  - Source: [NPR Training, "The journey from print to radio storytelling"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape)
- **Definite, specific, concrete** - William Strunk Jr., rule 12: prefer definite, specific, concrete language to the vague and general.
  - In a narration script: name the bakery, the price, the year, the person. "Prices rose sharply" becomes "A loaf that cost one coin on Monday cost two by Friday."
  - Source: [Strunk, The Elements of Style (1918), rule 12](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)

## 6. Narration voice and writing for the ear

- **If you wouldn't say it, don't write it** - NPR Training's mantra: "If you wouldn't say it that way, don't write it that way." Written words like "residents", "individuals" and "war-torn" turn a voice into a newsreader.
  - In a narration script: read every sentence aloud in the saved tone and swap written words for spoken ones ("people who live there" for "residents").
  - Source: [NPR Training, "The journey from print to radio storytelling"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape)
- **Subject next to verb, speaker before quote** - NPR Training: dependent clauses are "toxic to radio writing" ("My dad taught physics for 40 years. He's a smart guy."), and in speech the attribution comes before the quote.
  - In a narration script: break appositive clauses into their own sentences; this is also how the profile's 30-word limit gets met. TTS reads no quote marks, so name the speaker first: "The bank's chair said prices would settle by spring."
  - Source: [NPR Training, "The journey from print to radio storytelling"](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/05/30/g-s1-65814/the-journey-from-print-to-radio-storytelling-a-guide-for-navigating-a-new-landscape)
- **End the sentence on its new word** - William Strunk Jr., rule 18: the place for the words you want most prominent is usually the end.
  - In a narration script: end on the new information. "The town printed a billion dollars, and the bakery still had ten loaves" ends on the point. Read just the last words of each sentence in a chapter; they should trace the argument.
  - Source: [Strunk, The Elements of Style (1918), rule 18](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)
- **Short words, fewer words, fewer sentences** - George Orwell: "Never use a long word where a short one will do", cut any word you can, prefer everyday English to jargon. Kurt Vonnegut, "Have the guts to cut": scratch out a sentence that does not illuminate the subject in a new and useful way, however good it sounds.
  - In a narration script: in each chapter, find the sentence you like most that adds no new fact or turn, and cut it. A technical term stays only if the viewer needs it later, and gets a plain gloss in the same breath.
  - Sources: [Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946), rules ii, iii, v](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/); [Vonnegut, "How to Write With Style", IEEE Trans. Prof. Comm. PC-24(2), 1981, p. 66](https://kmh-lanl.hansonhub.com/pc-24-66-vonnegut.pdf)
- **Fresh figures or none** - George Orwell, rule i: never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech you are used to seeing in print.
  - In a narration script: build metaphors from the video's own through-line ("the baker's ledger") and drop stock ones ("tip of the iceberg", "a perfect storm"). One figure per chapter is plenty.
  - Source: [Orwell, "Politics and the English Language", rule i](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)
- **Add to the picture** - Billy Wilder: in voice-over, don't describe what the audience already sees. "Add to what they're seeing." Walter Murch warns that a guide who never lets people look for themselves ends up resented.
  - In a narration script: the `action` elements say what the viewer sees; the narration supplies what the picture cannot show: the cause, the number, the consequence, a person's reason. The pictures are made later and by someone else, so narrating them can also contradict them.
  - Sources: [Crowe, Conversations with Wilder (1999)](https://archive.org/details/conversationswit0000wild); [Murch, In the Blink of an Eye, 2nd ed. (2001)](https://www.silmanjamespress.com/shop/filmmaking-directing/in-the-blink-of-an-eye2nd-edition/)

## 7. Character and stakes (even in explainers)

- **Make me care, through people** - Andrew Stanton calls "Make me care" the greatest story commandment. Ken Burns told the Mayo Clinic his "films were about people, not institutions."
  - In a narration script: tell an institution's story through one person's decision at one moment, using a person from the chapter's `facts_to_verify` (an invented name is a P1 finding). "The economy" makes no one care; a baker deciding whether to raise her price does.
  - Sources: [Stanton, TED (2012)](https://www.ted.com/talks/andrew_stanton_the_clues_to_a_great_story); [Burns, "A Note from Ken Burns", PBS](https://www.pbs.org/kenburns/the-mayo-clinic/note-from-ken-burns)
- **Intention and obstacle** - Aaron Sorkin: somebody wants something, something stands in their way, and it must be hard to overcome; the tactics they use to get past it are the story.
  - In a narration script: even an explainer has an agent with a goal. "The central bank wants prices to rise two percent a year. Its main tool is the interest rate, and raising it puts people out of work." Name the want and the obstacle in the chapter where the agent first appears.
  - Sources: [Sorkin, OnWriting ep. 41 (2021)](https://www.wgaeast.org/onwriting/aaron-sorkin-the-trial-of-the-chicago-7/); [Sorkin, "What I've Learned", Esquire (2011)](https://www.esquire.com/entertainment/interviews/a9103/aaron-sorkin-interview-0111/)
- **Say what happens if they fail** - Emma Coats, story basic #16: what are the stakes, what happens if they don't succeed, stack the odds against them. Ira Glass passes on This American Life's rule for pitches: with nothing at stake there is no question driving the story forward.
  - In a narration script: state the cost of failure early, in terms the viewer can feel, before the outcome: "If the harvest fails, the town has nothing left to trade for bread."
  - Sources: [Coats, #storybasics 16 (2011)](https://x.com/lawnrocket/status/57834950585626624); [Glass, Transom manifesto part 3 (2004)](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/)

## 8. Endings and payoff

- **Action, then what it means** - Ira Glass: a story has two parts, the plot and moments of reflection where someone says something interesting about what happened; if the meaning is not big and surprising, the story falls flat.
  - In a narration script: after each anecdote or demonstration, one sentence says what it shows about how the world works, specific to this case. "So the extra money changed the price on the board and left the shelf the same" is a reflection; "This teaches us an important lesson" is a flagged generic one.
  - Source: [Glass, Transom manifesto part 2 (2004)](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/)
- **Pay off the hook in its own terms, then stop** - Aristotle: an end follows from what came before and nothing follows it. MrBeast's guide: the payoff at the end holds viewers. Billy Wilder's last tip on endings: "Don't hang around."
  - In a narration script: the last chapter answers ch01's question with ch01's own objects (the billion, the ten loaves), gives the one CTA, and ends. No recap; the profile flags "So to recap".
  - Sources: [Poetics, tr. Butcher, Part VII](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/poetics.1.1.html); [MrBeast guide](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/6623bf84e83241ec49b548e4/66edaa19db6e9359bb92931f_How-To-Succeed-At-MrBeast-Production%20(2).pdf); [Crowe, Conversations with Wilder](https://archive.org/details/conversationswit0000wild)

## 9. Revision

- **Tell it aloud before you write it** - Ira Glass: tell the story to friends and notice how you tell it, where you start, what you leave out; that order is often the best structure. If you can't tell it compellingly, you haven't worked out what it is about.
  - In a narration script: before drafting, speak the outline in about a minute as if to a friend. Keep the order you fell into, and cut the part you caught yourself rushing past.
  - Source: [Glass, Transom manifesto part 2 (2004)](https://transom.org/2004/ira-glass/)
- **Kill the weak parts whole** - Ira Glass: This American Life kills between a third and a half of the stories it tries, because recorded material is "trying to be crap" unless you prop it up at every stage.
  - In a narration script: when a draft runs long, cut the weakest beat or chapter whole before trimming words evenly everywhere.
  - Source: [Glass, "Ira Glass on Storytelling" part 2, 0:58-2:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dx2cI-2FJRs)
- **Cut by Murch's order** - Walter Murch's Rule of Six ranks what a cut must keep: emotion first, then story, rhythm, eye-trace, planarity, spatial continuity; sacrifice from the bottom up. What the audience remembers "is how they felt."
  - In a narration script: when a chapter runs past 150 s, cut lines that serve rhythm or decoration before lines that carry feeling or move the explanation.
  - Source: [Murch, In the Blink of an Eye, 2nd ed. (2001), "The Rule of Six"](https://www.silmanjamespress.com/shop/filmmaking-directing/in-the-blink-of-an-eye2nd-edition/)
- **Read the retention graph as notes** - YouTube Help: dips mark where viewers skipped or left; spikes can mean rewatching because something was unclear; top moments that come late should move earlier.
  - In a narration script: once a published video has data, map dips and spikes to chapters and carry the lesson into the next outline.
  - Source: [YouTube Help, "Measure key moments for audience retention"](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415)

## Revision checklist

1. Can the video be stated as "a story about X, and what's interesting is Y", with a Y most viewers would not guess?
2. Does every outline hand-off join with "therefore" or "but" rather than "and then"?
3. Would cutting any chapter change the ending? (Any chapter where the answer is no gets merged or cut.)
4. Do the first ~75 words show or name what the title and thumbnail promise?
5. Does the hook still read as honest after the last chapter is heard?
6. Is there one long question for the video and one short question in each chapter?
7. Does each chapter have a moment where the obvious prediction fails?
8. Does every setup (question, character, number) have a payoff, and every payoff a setup?
9. If the topic carries a common misconception, is it stated fairly before it is corrected?
10. Does each term arrive after the viewer has seen what it names?
11. Is there at most one number per sentence, rounded and compared to something?
12. Does someone in the script want something, face an obstacle, and risk a stated cost?
13. Does the narration add to the picture instead of describing it?
14. Does each anecdote end in a specific reflection, and does the last chapter answer ch01's question with ch01's objects?
15. Read aloud, does every sentence sound spoken, with its subject near its verb and its new word last?
