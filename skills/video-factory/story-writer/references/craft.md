# Craft for prose fiction

What turns a settled story bible into a story people finish. Each principle names its source; the line under it says what to do in this pipeline. The story will be adapted into a film next, so the principles that keep it concrete also make it easier to film.

## Structure

- **Therefore and but, never "and then"** - Trey Parker and Matt Stone (NYU guest lecture, 2011): if the beats of an outline join with "and then", the story is a list; join them with "therefore" or "but".
  - Read the outline's "turn" column aloud with those words between rows. An "and then" row is merged, cut, or given a cause.
- **Start as close to the end as possible** - Kurt Vonnegut, eight rules (preface to *Bagombo Snuff Box*, 1999).
  - Chapter 1 opens on the day things change, not on backstory. Backstory arrives when a scene needs it, through the flashbacks the bible lists.
- **Every sentence reveals character or advances the action** - Vonnegut, same rules.
  - A paragraph that does neither is cut, however good it sounds.
- **What is set up pays off** - Anton Chekhov's rifle on the wall (his letters, and Ilia Gurliand's 1904 recollection): what is shown early must matter later.
  - Every object, rule or skill the story lingers on is used by the end; whatever is used at the climax is planted at least one chapter earlier.
- **Know the ending first** - Edgar Allan Poe, "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846): plot only makes sense written with the ending in view.
  - The bible fixes the ending before any chapter; each chapter is checked against it.

## Scenes

- **Goal, conflict, disaster; then reaction, dilemma, decision** - Dwight V. Swain, *Techniques of the Selling Writer* (1965): a scene is a character pursuing a goal into an obstacle; the sequel is the emotional fallout and the choice that starts the next scene.
  - Every scene has a character who wants something now. If nobody wants anything in a scene, it becomes a summary paragraph.
- **Leave out the part readers skip** - Elmore Leonard, "Easy on the Adverbs, Exclamation Points and Especially Hooptedoodle" (*The New York Times*, 2001).
  - Cut travel, waking up, weather openings and recaps unless something happens in them.

## Viewpoint and voice

- **Choose the viewpoint on purpose and hold it** - Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft* (1998), on point of view and voice.
  - One viewpoint character per scene; the narration knows only what that character could know. Change viewpoint only at a scene break, as the outline says.
- **Show the body, not the label** - the screenwriting habit that also serves prose: "She was afraid" tells; "She stopped. She did not turn around." lets the reader feel it.
  - Name an emotion only when the action cannot carry it. Inner thought stays, but tie it to something seen or heard: the film will need that hook.
- **Dialogue does two jobs** - Leonard again: "said" carries dialogue; the line itself carries the feeling.
  - Use "said" and "asked"; no adverbs on dialogue tags. People talk past each other and leave things out.

## Revision checklist

Run before the review step and fix what fails:

- The premise line still describes the story as written.
- Each chapter ends on a turn; the turns join with "but" or "therefore".
- Every setup pays off and every payoff was set up.
- No chapter holds a viewpoint slip, a recap or a weather opening.
- Every named character has a job; every world rule is used and never broken.
- The last scene shows what changed, without a summary of the lesson.
